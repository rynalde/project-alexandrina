<Body>

### 1. The usual recipe: input → label

A classifier learns a mapping you supervise. Photo of a cat → "cat". It only knows what is in your label set, and every example costs a human a few seconds of labeling.

That ceiling is the whole problem. ImageNet took years and millions of dollars to label, while there are billions of hours of unlabeled video sitting on the internet doing nothing.

LeCun made the same point with a back-of-the-envelope estimate in January 2024: by age four a child has taken in about 10¹⁵ bytes through the eyes alone — by his count, some 50 times more than the text the biggest language models were trained on. Most of what we know about the world was never labeled.

### 2. The other recipe: input → what comes next

A world model learns a different mapping. Show it frames 1–8 of a video and ask for frame 9. Show it an image with a hole and ask what is behind the hole.

Nobody wrote a label — <Highlight>frame 9 already exists in the file</Highlight>. The data supervises itself. This is <TermTip term="self-supervised learning">the target is cut out of the input itself — no human writes it</TermTip>, and it is what unlocks all that unlabeled video.

### 3. Why prediction forces understanding

Before reading on, play the game yourself:

<NextFrameGuess />

To guess frame 9 above chance, the network has no choice but to internalize how the world behaves: objects do not teleport, a ball in flight keeps going and curves down, an occluded arm still exists behind the torso, a person mid-jump comes back down.

None of that was ever labeled. It is the *only* way to reduce the prediction error. That accumulated know-how — physics, permanence, causality — is what we call the <Highlight>world model</Highlight>.

### 4. The twist that trips people up

<PredictReveal prompt="Training is over. The model predicts frame 9 very well. What do you think we actually keep and reuse?">
Not the predictions. We keep the encoder — the part that turned frames into a compact description of what's happening. The predicted frames get thrown away.
</PredictReveal>

After training, we usually **throw the prediction away**. The predicted frame was never the point. The point is the internal representation the encoder learned in order to make that prediction — a compact vector that captures "what is going on here".

There are always two tasks:

- — <Highlight>Pretext task</Highlight>: the fake job used for training (predict the missing part). Disposable.
- — <Highlight>Downstream task</Highlight>: the real job you care about (classify the action, detect a fall, plan a robot's next move).

### 5. Where the name was popularised

Ha &amp; Schmidhuber (2018) learned compressed models of game environments (a racing game and VizDoom). In VizDoom they went further: the controller was trained *entirely inside the model's own dream* of the game, then transferred back to the real one. Their paper popularised the term world model (the idea goes back to Schmidhuber's work around 1990).

Meta's JEPA family (2023 onward) is the modern version, scaled to real video — and that is where V-JEPA lives. The field is still moving fast: chapter 04's timeline runs to 2026, and in chapter 08 you build a small one yourself.

</Body>

<Callout icon={Brain} title="one sentence to keep">
  A world model is a network that learns physics and common sense by predicting the future, not by memorizing labels — and what we keep afterwards is the representation, not the prediction.
</Callout>

<Marginalia label="traps">
  "World model = generative model" — no, generation is one use, not the definition. "Self-supervised = unsupervised" — no, there IS a target, it comes from the data. "The goal is an accurate prediction" — no, the goal is the representation. Mixing up pretext and downstream tasks.
</Marginalia>
