### The usual recipe: input → label

A classifier learns a mapping you supervise: photo of a cat → "cat". It only knows the classes in your label set, and every example costs a human a few seconds of labeling.

That is the ceiling. ImageNet took years of crowd-sourced work to label, while billions of hours of unlabeled video sit on the internet doing nothing.

LeCun put a number on it in January 2024: by age four, a child has taken in about 10¹⁵ bytes through the eyes alone — by his count, some 50 times more than the text the biggest language models were trained on. Most of what we know about the world was never labeled.

### The other recipe: input → what comes next

A world model learns a different mapping. Show it frames 1–8 of a video and ask for frame 9. Show it an image with a hole and ask what is behind the hole.

Nobody wrote a label — <Highlight>frame 9 already exists in the file</Highlight>. The data supervises itself. This is <TermTip term="self-supervised learning">the target is cut out of the input itself — no human writes it</TermTip>, and it is what unlocks all that unlabeled video.

### Why prediction forces understanding

The game you just played is the whole trick. To guess the next frame better than chance, a network has to internalize how the world behaves: objects don't teleport, a thrown ball keeps going and curves down, an arm hidden behind a torso still exists.

None of that was labeled. It is simply the *only* way to reduce the prediction error. That accumulated know-how — physics, permanence, cause and effect — is what we call the <Highlight>world model</Highlight>.

### The twist: we throw the prediction away

<PredictReveal prompt="Training is over. The model predicts frame 9 very well. What do you think we actually keep and reuse?">
Not the predictions. We keep the encoder — the part that turned frames into a compact description of what's happening. The predicted frames get thrown away.
</PredictReveal>

The predicted frame was never the point. The point is the representation the encoder learned in order to make it: a compact vector that captures "what is going on here". So there are always two tasks:

- <Highlight>Pretext task</Highlight>: the training game (predict the missing part). Disposable.
- <Highlight>Downstream task</Highlight>: the job you care about (classify an action, detect a fall, plan a robot's next move).

### Where the name comes from

Ha &amp; Schmidhuber (2018) popularised the term. They learned compressed models of two games, a racing game and VizDoom — and in VizDoom they trained the controller *entirely inside the model's own dream* before moving it back to the real game.

Meta's JEPA family (2023 onward) is the modern version, scaled to real video, and that is where V-JEPA lives. Chapter 04 follows the family to 2026; in chapter 08 you build a small one yourself.
