### The cheat

<PredictReveal prompt="The prediction and the target both come out of learned encoders. What's the laziest way to make them match — for every input, forever?">
Make the encoder output the same vector no matter what it sees, and have the predictor output that same vector. Prediction = target, always. Loss = 0. And the representation is useless, because it says nothing about the input.
</PredictReveal>

That failure has a name: <Highlight>representation collapse</Highlight>. The loss looks perfect and the model has learned nothing.

### Why pixel models never collapse

Pixels are fixed data. The model can't change what frame 9 looks like, so it can't make the target trivial. Moving the target inside the model (chapter 02) is exactly what opens the door.

### Defense 1 — contrastive: push things apart

Pull two views of the *same* input together, and push *different* inputs apart. Those "different inputs" are called **negatives**. A constant output can't push anything apart, so it's punished. Examples: SimCLR, MoCo (2020). Cost: you need lots of negatives, usually big batches.

### Defense 2 — regularize: demand variety

VICReg (Bardes, Ponce &amp; LeCun, 2022) adds three terms:

- — **Variance**: each embedding dimension must keep a minimum spread across the batch. A constant has zero spread.
- — **Invariance**: two views of the same input should match.
- — **Covariance**: different dimensions should not carry the same information.

### Defense 3 — asymmetry: a slow-moving, gradient-free teacher

BYOL (2020) showed you can skip negatives entirely by making the two sides *different*:

- — a **predictor** sits on the online side only;
- — the target side gets a <TermTip term="stop-gradient">treat the value as a constant: no gradient flows back through it</TermTip>;
- — the target encoder is an **EMA** (a slow moving average) of the online encoder's weights.

This is the defense **I-JEPA and V-JEPA use**. (SimSiam, 2021, later showed the EMA isn't strictly required — stop-gradient plus a predictor is enough.) Honest note: *why* this asymmetry prevents collapse is still being studied. It works reliably in practice; the full theory is younger than the trick.

### How you'll check for it in your own lab

Two cheap alarms for vol. xii: log the **standard deviation of each embedding dimension** across a batch (it should not sink toward 0), and run a quick **linear probe** now and then (stuck at chance = nothing learned).

</Body>

<Callout icon={ShieldAlert} title="one sentence to keep">
  If both sides of the loss are learned, the model can win by outputting a constant; contrastive negatives, variance regularization, or an asymmetric EMA-teacher-plus-predictor design block that cheat — JEPA uses the asymmetric one.
</Callout>

<Marginalia label="traps">
  Low loss is not proof of learning — collapse gives zero loss. Pixel-reconstruction models don't collapse this way; the risk comes from learned targets. BYOL/JEPA need no negatives. EMA alone is not the whole story — the predictor and stop-gradient are part of the asymmetry.
</Marginalia>
