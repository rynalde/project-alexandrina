<Body>

### Start from one input, split in two

Take one clip: 9 frames of a ball bouncing. Hide frame 9. Now you have two pieces of the **same** input:

- — <Highlight>Context</Highlight>: what the model is allowed to see (frames 1–8).
- — <Highlight>Target</Highlight>: what it must guess (frame 9).

The rule that decides what gets hidden is called the <Highlight>mask</Highlight>. Hide the future and you get "predict what's next". Hide random blocks and you get "fill in the gap". Same machine, different mask.

### Ingredient 1 — the encoder

The encoder turns raw input into a <TermTip term="representation">a list of numbers (a vector) that describes the input — similar inputs get similar vectors</TermTip>: a compact summary of what is going on.

Think of it as the eyes plus the part of the brain that says "a ball, moving right, falling". It throws away what doesn't matter (exact pixel noise) and keeps what does (position, velocity, identity).

In notation: <Kbd>z = f(x)</Kbd> — input `x` in, representation `z` out.

### Ingredient 2 — the predictor

The predictor takes the representation of the context and guesses the target.

It is the imagination: "given a ball moving right and falling, frame 9 should show it lower and further right". It needs to know **where/when** the target is, so it also receives the target's position (which frame, which patch).

In notation: <Kbd>ẑ = g(z_context, where)</Kbd>.

### Ingredient 3 — the loss

The loss measures how wrong the guess was. It compares the prediction with the real target and returns one number. Training = repeatedly nudging the encoder and predictor weights to make that number smaller.

The big design decision hides here: **what exactly is "the real target"?**

- — Raw pixels of frame 9 → a pixel-prediction model.
- — The *representation* of frame 9 → a latent-prediction model (this is JEPA).

That single choice changes everything. It is the whole of the next chapter.

<PredictReveal prompt="Could you train the encoder with no loss at all?">
No. An encoder only learns when a loss tells it how wrong something was. In predictive architectures like BYOL and JEPA, that loss is computed on a predictor's output. (Contrastive and VICReg-style methods skip the predictor and compare two embeddings directly — but there is always a loss.)
</PredictReveal>

### The loop

1. Mask the input → context + target.
2. Encoder turns the context into `z_context`.
3. Predictor guesses the target from `z_context`.
4. Loss compares guess vs target.
5. Backpropagate, update weights, repeat millions of times.

In chapter 08 you write each of these in a few lines of PyTorch and run the loop yourself.

### Which part do you keep?

<QuickCheck
  q="Pretraining is done. Which part goes into your downstream classifier?"
  options={["The predictor", "The encoder", "The mask", "The loss"]}
  correct={1}
  why="The encoder learned to see. The predictor was scaffolding for the training game."
/>

Usually the **encoder**. It is what learned to see. The predictor was scaffolding for the training game. (Nuance for later: for robot *planning*, V-JEPA 2 keeps the frozen encoder and trains a **new**, action-conditioned predictor. The pretraining predictor is still thrown away.)

</Body>

<Callout icon={Layers} title="one sentence to keep">
  Encoder = sees and summarizes. Predictor = imagines the hidden part. Loss = says how wrong the imagination was. The mask decides what is hidden, and the loss target decides whether you predict pixels or meaning.
</Callout>

<Marginalia label="traps">
  Context and target are NOT input and label — they are two parts of the same input. The predictor is usually discarded, the encoder is kept. The encoder can't learn without a loss; in JEPA-style models that loss is computed on the predictor's output. Choosing the loss target (pixels vs representations) is the most important design decision.
</Marginalia>
