### One input, split in two

Take one clip: 9 frames of a ball bouncing. Hide frame 9. Now you have two pieces of the **same** input:

- <Highlight>Context</Highlight>: what the model may see (frames 1–8).
- <Highlight>Target</Highlight>: what it must guess (frame 9).

The rule that decides what gets hidden is the <Highlight>mask</Highlight>. Hide the future and you get "predict what's next". Hide random blocks and you get "fill in the gap". Same machine, different mask.

### The whole machine

Press play, or step through it. The next three screens zoom into each part.

<IngredientsFigure />

### Ingredient 1 — the encoder

The encoder turns raw input into a <TermTip term="representation">a list of numbers (a vector) that describes the input — similar inputs get similar vectors</TermTip>: a compact summary of what is going on.

Think of the eyes plus the part of the brain that says "a ball, moving right, falling". It drops what doesn't matter (pixel noise) and keeps what does (position, velocity, identity). In notation: <Kbd>z = f(x)</Kbd>.

### Ingredient 2 — the predictor

The predictor takes the context's representation and guesses the target. It is the imagination: "a ball moving right and falling should be lower and further right in frame 9".

It must also know **where or when** the target is, so it receives the target's position (which frame, which patch). In notation: <Kbd>ẑ = g(z_context, where)</Kbd>.

### Ingredient 3 — the loss

The loss compares the guess with the real target and returns one number: how wrong. Training nudges the encoder and predictor weights, millions of times, to make that number smaller.

The big design decision hides here: **what exactly is "the real target"?**

- The raw pixels of frame 9 → a pixel-prediction model.
- The *representation* of frame 9 → a latent-prediction model. This is JEPA.

That one choice changes everything, and it is the whole of the next chapter.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: is there always a predictor?</summary>

There is always a loss — an encoder only learns when something tells it how wrong it was. In predictive architectures like BYOL and JEPA, that loss is computed on a predictor's output. Contrastive and VICReg-style methods skip the predictor and compare two embeddings directly (chapter 03).

</details>

### The loop

1. Mask the input → context + target.
2. The encoder turns the context into `z_context`.
3. The predictor guesses the target from `z_context`.
4. The loss compares guess and target.
5. Backpropagate, update the weights, repeat.

In chapter 08 you write each line of this loop in PyTorch and run it.

Usually the **encoder**. One nuance for later: for robot *planning*, V-JEPA 2 keeps the frozen encoder and trains a **new**, action-conditioned predictor. The pretraining predictor is still thrown away.
