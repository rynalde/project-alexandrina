<Body>

### Keep the encoder, usually frozen

After pretraining you keep the encoder (chapter 01) and — most of the time — **freeze** it. Testing a frozen encoder is the fair test of a world model: it measures what it learned on its own, not what a fine-tuning run taught it afterwards.

### Option 1 — linear probe

Average all the patch tokens into one vector, train a single linear layer on top. Cheapest possible read-out: a fast sanity check, with a lower ceiling.

### Option 2 — attentive probe

V-JEPA outputs one feature per tubelet — **thousands of tokens** (1,568 for a 16-frame, 224px clip) and **no special "class" token**. The paper also points out that nothing in the feature-prediction objective makes the features *linearly* separable, and averaging lets the background drown out the action. So the attentive probe learns a **query token** that cross-attends over all the tokens, adds the result back to the query (residual), and passes it through a small MLP. Only the probe is trained.

- — **V-JEPA**: one cross-attention layer + a 2-layer MLP.
- — **V-JEPA 2**: a deeper probe — 4 transformer blocks, the last one using cross-attention with the learnable query.

<AttentivePoolViz />

### Option 3 — fine-tune

Unfreeze everything and train on labeled data. Best accuracy, highest cost, easy to overfit on small datasets.

### Option 4 — plan with it (V-JEPA 2-AC)

V-JEPA 2 goes past classification. Keep the encoder frozen, then train a **new** ~300M-parameter **action-conditioned predictor** on **less than 62 hours** of unlabeled robot video (DROID), where it learns: given the current state and an action, what comes next.

To reach a goal shown in an image, the robot runs a loop:

  title="Planning in latent space"
  steps={[
    "Encode the current camera frame and the goal image.",
    "Sample many candidate action sequences (Cross-Entropy Method).",
    "Imagine each sequence's outcome with the predictor — in representation space, no pixels.",
    "Score each: L1 distance between the imagined final state and the goal representation.",
    "Execute only the first action of the optimized sequence.",
    "Observe, then replan from the new state (receding horizon).",
  ]}
/>

Deployed zero-shot on Franka arms in two labs, it did reach and pick-and-place from image goals (for pick-and-place it was also given two sub-goal images along the way): 80% success with a cup and 65% with a box averaged over both labs, against 15% and 10% for the Octo baseline. It planned one step ahead at a time: 800 sampled actions, 10 CEM refinements, about **16 seconds per action** on one RTX 4090. In the lab where the pixel world model Cosmos was also tried, Cosmos needed about **4 minutes** per action with 80 samples and scored 0% on pick-and-place.

Why the sub-goals? Planning far ahead is hard: prediction errors pile up and the number of possible action sequences explodes. So V-JEPA 2-AC plans greedily, one step at a time — and a long task like pick-and-place is not greedy. Sub-goal images turn it into a chain of short, greedy hops. (The paper describes its energy landscape as relatively smooth and locally convex, which should make such short hops easy to plan.) The next screen shows the greedy trap with a wall; chapter 08's lab finds a second cause: its latent distance stops tracking real distance beyond about 9–12px.

### Option 5 — measure surprise

A world model also tells you when something *doesn't* make sense. Show it a clip, let it predict the next frames, and measure the error in representation space: that error is its **surprise**. Garrido et al. (2025) used exactly this on V-JEPA with pairs of possible and impossible clips — for example, objects that vanish or change shape. V-JEPA was more surprised by the impossible clips on properties like object permanence and shape consistency; pixel-prediction models and multimodal LLMs stayed closer to chance. That is how "the model understands object permanence" becomes a number.

<PredictReveal prompt="For your dissertation you'll use a frozen V-JEPA 2 with an attentive probe on a free Colab GPU. Why frozen instead of fine-tuned?">
A large ViT seeing 2,048–8,192 tokens per clip (V-JEPA 2 at 256px, 16–64 frames) won't fit training gradients on a T4. Frozen, you can run the encoder once, cache the features to Drive, and train only the small probe — fast and cheap. It's also the fair, standard way to evaluate what the world model learned.

<Callout icon={Crosshair} title="one sentence to keep">
  Freeze the encoder and read it out with a linear probe (fast check), an attentive probe (V-JEPA's standard — a learned query cross-attending over patch tokens), or fine-tune for maximum accuracy; V-JEPA 2-AC goes further and plans actions by imagining outcomes in latent space (with sub-goals for long tasks), and prediction error doubles as a surprise meter for impossible events.
</Callout>

<Marginalia label="traps">
  Frozen evaluation doesn't mean nothing is trained — the probe is. An attentive probe is not fine-tuning. V-JEPA 2-AC plans in representation space; it never generates pixels. For planning, V-JEPA 2-AC trains a NEW action-conditioned predictor on the frozen encoder — the pretraining predictor is not reused. Its energy scores the imagined END state. Surprise is prediction error, not a classifier.
</Marginalia>
